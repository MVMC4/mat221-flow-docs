title: Integration by Substitution
description: Reverse the chain rule by matching an inside function with its differential.

Substitution reverses the chain rule. Look for $f(g(x))g'(x)$, choose $u=g(x)$, and rewrite the integral completely in $u$.

## Rule and decision

$$
\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du.
$$

Differentiate $u$, multiply by $dx$, solve for the exact differential piece present, and do not leave an $x$ behind.

## Worked example: direct match

For $\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx$, choose $u=x^2$, so $du=2x\,dx$. The integral becomes $\int\cos u\,du=\sin u+C$, hence $\sin(x^2)+C$. Differentiate to check the chain rule.

## Worked example: constant mismatch

For $\int x\sqrt{x+1}\,dx$, choose $u=x+1$. Then $du=dx$ and $x=u-1$, so:

$$
\int(u-1)\sqrt{u}\,du.
$$

Integrate the two powers and substitute back. The leftover $x$ was not ignored; it was rewritten as $u-1$.

<div className="soft-callout"><strong>Transfer.</strong> Try the same structure with $u=4-x^2$. The limits reverse from $4$ to $0$, so keep the sign visible.</div>

## Definite substitution

For definite integrals, change the limits when $u$ is introduced. In $\int_0^1 x/(1+x^2)\,dx$, $u=1+x^2$ changes the limits $1\to2$, and the answer is $\frac12\ln2$.

<div className="soft-callout"><strong>Common error.</strong> Evaluating a $u$-antiderivative at $x$-limits mixes variables and usually loses a constant factor.</div>
