An antiderivative of $f$ is a function $F$ with $F'(x)=f(x)$. Differentiation forgets constants, so integration returns a family:

$$
\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C.
$$

## Differentiation undoes accumulation

FTC Part 1 says:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=f(x).
$$

The moving upper limit is the key. No antiderivative is needed.

<div className="soft-callout"><strong>Worked example.</strong> For $\frac{d}{dx}\int_2^x(3t^2+1)\,dt$, evaluate the integrand at the moving edge: $3x^2+1$. If the upper limit is $x^2$, use the chain rule and multiply by $2x$.</div>

## Evaluation by an antiderivative

FTC Part 2 says:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).
$$

For $\int_1^3 2x\,dx$, take $F(x)=x^2$, then $F(3)-F(1)=9-1=8$. The constant disappears because it is subtracted at both bounds.

## The power rule backwards

Raise the exponent, divide by the new exponent, and add $C$. The exception is $n=-1$:

$$
\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C,\qquad \int\frac{dx}{x}=\ln|x|+C.
$$

For $\int 3x^2\,dx$, raise $2$ to $3$, divide $3x^3$ by $3$, and check by differentiating $x^3+C$.

<div className="soft-callout"><strong>Common error.</strong> Never drop $+C$ from an indefinite integral, and never use the power rule at $n=-1$.</div>
